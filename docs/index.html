VIGIL

Vibration Intelligence Grid for Integrity and Lifecycle

Signal processingOctober 2026Seva First Innovation Challenge, Defencegithub.com/amayakavya/vigil

A clip-on sensor that tells a mechanic which part of a generator, pump or motor is wearing out, days before it fails. It learns each machine from its own first day of running, names the failing part from the bearing's dimensions, and needs no internet connection. Everything below was measured on public test-rig data, and the method itself runs on this page, on raw recordings or on a file of your own.

What it found

NASA's IMS rig ran four bearings on one shaft until one failed, three times over. VIGIL saw only the first 24 hours of each bearing before it started judging, and it was never shown a failure. Four bearings failed across the three runs.

A warning counts as sustained once the alarm never goes quiet for more than 12 hours again. The comparison is with what a workshop could set up without VIGIL: an alarm when the overall vibration level doubles, or rises by 35%, on the same snapshots with the same three-readings-in-a-row rule. Those alarms never name a part.

Three things in that table are weaker than they look, and they are stated here so nobody has to find them. Bearing 4 in run 1 was first flagged 603 hours before the stop, but on and off: only from 159 hours out did the warning hold. After each fault began, eight healthy bearings on the same shafts also alarmed, every time later than the failing one, and in every case the failing bearing's sensor was the loudest on the shaft at that moment, so the system points to it. And in run 3 two healthy bearings crossed the alarm level on seven single readings before any fault. The three-in-a-row rule, fixed before run 3 was scored, is what kept them quiet.

Naming, on a second dataset

Case Western Reserve University cut faults of 0.18 to 0.53 mm into test bearings and recorded them at four loads: 52 recordings. VIGIL names a part only when one defect line stands at least 10 dB clear of its neighbourhood, and otherwise declines. This set was used while the method was built, so it shows what the method can do, not a blind test.

Drag through a run and read the workshop board as a mechanic would have seen it at that hour. A warning stays on the board until a mechanic clears it, as it would in a workshop, so a bearing whose readings later dip below the alarm keeps its warning.

How it decides

Learn

For its first day on a machine the node records one second of vibration every ten minutes. From those snapshots it takes the median and the spread of six readings: overall level, kurtosis, crest factor, and the strength of the outer race, inner race and rolling element lines. That is the machine's own normal. It needs no failure data from anywhere, which matters because a government workshop keeps almost none.

Watch

Every later snapshot is scored against that normal, as the worst robust z-score of the six readings. Each reading has a floor on its spread: 5% on level, 0.1 on kurtosis and crest factor, 1 dB on a line, the smallest change a sensor re-seated on its magnet can claim to see. An alarm needs a score of 6 or more on three readings in a row, so a single knock does not page anyone. Readings taken with the machine stopped are ignored.

Name

A spall on a bearing race is struck by each rolling element in turn, at a rate fixed by the bearing's geometry. For n elements of diameter d on a pitch diameter D, at contact angle φ and shaft frequency f:

outer race = (n/2) f (1 − (d/D) cos φ)
inner race = (n/2) f (1 + (d/D) cos φ)
rolling element = (D/2d) f (1 − ((d/D) cos φ)²)

The node flattens the spectrum so shaft and mains lines stop masking the bearing, finds the band where the impacts ring loudest, takes the squared envelope of that band, and measures how far each predicted line stands above its neighbourhood. The asset register holds each point's bearing number, so the board can say which bearing, which part of it, and which spare to draw.

What failed first

The first version did not work, twice. On the Case Western set it named only 26 of 52, because its band search kept choosing the band just under the sampling limit, where the anti-alias filter leaves impulsive noise. Keeping the search between 200 Hz and the sensor's 6 kHz limit, in bands at least 1 kHz wide, took it to 40. On NASA run 2 it then alarmed on all three healthy bearings, on rises in overall level of 7 to 14% against a first day so steady that changes that small scored as faults. The spread floors and the stopped-machine rule above fixed that. Runs 1 and 3 were scored once, after both fixes, with nothing changed since.

What it is not, yet

  • The node is designed, not built. Its parts are an ESP32-S3 microcontroller and ST's IIS3DWB vibration accelerometer, whose response is within 3 dB up to 6 kHz, the band every result here was computed within. The bench build starts in November 2026.
  • Nothing here has run on a police or forces machine. Test rigs are cleaner than a generator room.
  • A machine that is already damaged on installation would teach the node a damaged normal. The plan is to check each new machine against identical machines in the fleet and against the absolute vibration zones of ISO 20816, which is not yet built.
  • The method needs the shaft speed. Generators, pumps and motors run at a fixed speed. Vehicles do not, so they wait until speed tracking from the signal itself is built and tested on a bench.
  • Bluetooth is a radio. Where a site allows no transmitters, the node keeps its results and they are read over a cable.

The pilot it is built for

Six months at one Maharashtra Police establishment, a district headquarters or a State Reserve Police Force group campus, with about 60 measuring points on its generators, pumps, motors and compressors, for ₹10 lakh. That is 0.73% of the ₹13.73 crore the state gave its police for modernisation in 2023-24, the latest year the Bureau of Police Research and Development reports, and a tentative figure.
